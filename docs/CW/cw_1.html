Classwork 1

Reverse Engineering a Data-Generating Process

Learning Objectives

By the end of this activity, students will be able to:

  • Distinguish between a population process and an observed dataset.
  • Identify deterministic (explained) and random (unexplained) components of a data-generating process.
  • Explain how sampling, measurement error, and missing data can affect results.
  • Identify potential confounding variables.
  • Connect a data-generating process to an appropriate study design.

Scenario

A researcher collects the following information from 200 college students:

  • Hours studied during the week before an exam
  • Hours of sleep the night before the exam
  • Whether the student attended a review session
  • Previous mathematics GPA
  • Exam score

The resulting dataset shows that students who studied more hours generally earned higher exam scores.

Part A: Build the Data-Generating Process

Create a diagram showing how the observed data might have been generated. Your diagram must include:

  • Student background characteristics
  • Study behavior
  • Sleep
  • Exam performance
  • Measurement or reporting error

You may use the following diagram as a starting point. Add, remove, or rearrange connections when appropriate.

Alternative text: Student background may influence study hours, review-session attendance, and exam scores. Study hours, review-session attendance, and sleep may also influence exam scores. Measurement error and missing data affect the final recorded dataset.

Questions

  1. Which variables occur before the students begin studying for the exam?
  2. Which arrows represent possible causal relationships?
  3. What additional variables might influence both study hours and exam scores?
  4. How does the real-world process differ from the final recorded dataset?

Part B: Identify Sources of Variation

For each source from Part A, decide whether it is primarily systematic (explained), random (unexplained), or could be both. Briefly explain how it might affect the observed relationship between study hours and exam scores.

Part C: Evaluate a Causal Claim

Consider the following claim:

Increasing a student’s study time will cause the student’s exam score to increase.

Discuss the claim with your group and answer the following questions:

  1. Does the observed association establish that studying more causes higher exam scores? Explain.
  2. Which variables could confound the relationship between study hours and exam scores?
  3. Could reverse causation be present? For example, could a student’s expected exam performance influence how long the student studies?
  4. How might inaccurate reports of study hours affect the observed association?
  5. How could the study be redesigned to provide stronger evidence about causation?

Part D: Modify the Process

Choose one of the following changes to the study:

  1. Students are randomly assigned to a study-support program.
  2. Study hours are recorded using tracking software instead of self-reports.
  3. Only students with complete data are included in the analysis.
  4. The sample includes students from only one mathematics course.

For your selected change, explain its effect on each consideration below.

Consideration Effect of the proposed change
Bias
Variability
Causal interpretation
Generalizability

Then revise your data-generating-process diagram to show the change.

Closing Reflection

In two or three sentences, explain the difference between:

  1. The real-world process that produces student outcomes, and
  2. The process that produces the dataset researchers eventually analyze.
